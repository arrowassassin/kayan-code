# Your story bank

Eight prepared stories cover essentially every behavioral question you'll
be asked — most questions are one of the chapter-70 archetypes wearing
different clothes, and a good story flexes to fit several of them. This
chapter is a worksheet: for each slot, mining prompts to dig the story out
of your memory, a STAR skeleton to fill in writing, and the **senior
upgrade** — the one change that turns the same events from an L4 telling
into an L5 telling. Fill these in a document you actually keep; writing
them down is the difference between a story and a hope.

Each skeleton is the same shape — fill every line, in first person:

```
SITUATION (2 sentences): context + stakes
TASK (1 sentence): my specific responsibility
ACTIONS (3-5 bullets): I did X → I did Y → I did Z, incl. one hard call
RESULT (1-2 sentences): quantified outcome
REFLECTION (1 sentence): what I changed going forward
```

## Slot 1 — Conflict with a peer

**Mine it:** A design review that got heated. A PR debate that went three
rounds. A time you were sure a teammate's approach would fail. A time *you*
turned out to be wrong.
**Story must show:** disagreeing with the idea, not the person; bringing
data or a prototype instead of volume; a clean resolution — including
"disagree and commit" if you lost.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 tells "we argued, my design won." L5 tells "I
figured out what constraint *they* were optimizing for, found the option
that satisfied both, and the relationship got stronger" — the win is the
alignment, not the argument.

## Slot 2 — Conflict with your manager / pushing back up

**Mine it:** A deadline you believed was impossible. A directive to cut a
corner you thought was load-bearing. A priority call you disagreed with.
**Story must show:** respectful, evidenced pushback; escalation through
argument, not around people; committing fully once the decision landed.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 pushes back on their own task. L5 changes the
decision with evidence — "I brought the incident data, proposed a scoped
alternative, and the plan changed" — or commits and *mitigates the risk
anyway* when the call went the other way.

## Slot 3 — Ambiguity

**Mine it:** A project that started as one vague sentence. Requirements
that flipped mid-flight. Being the first person on an empty problem.
**Story must show:** you creating structure — asking who the user is,
writing the one-pager, timeboxing a prototype, cutting scope for a v1 —
rather than waiting for clarity to be issued.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 handles ambiguity in their task. L5 removes it for
*other people*: "I wrote the doc that became the team's definition of the
problem." The artifact that de-fogged everyone else is the L5 evidence.

## Slot 4 — Influence without authority

**Mine it:** Convincing another team to adopt/change something. Driving a
standard nobody asked you to drive. Getting a dependency prioritized on a
roadmap you didn't own.
**Story must show:** *whose behavior changed*, and the mechanism —
prototype, data, listening to their constraints first — not seniority or
nagging.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 convinces a teammate. L5 moves a group they have no
power over, and names the empathy step: "their pushback was about their
on-call load, so I built the migration tooling first — then they said yes."

## Slot 5 — Failure you own

**Mine it:** An outage or data issue you caused. A wrong technical bet
that cost the team a quarter. A time you sat on a concern silently and it
bit. Pick something with real cost — a safe fake failure fails the round.
**Story must show:** plain first-person ownership ("I shipped it without a
rollback plan"), fast honest handling, and a *systemic* fix, not just
regret.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 learns a personal lesson. L5 turns the failure into
infrastructure: "I ran the postmortem and the check I built now gates every
deploy on the team." The blast radius of the learning is the level.

## Slot 6 — Mentoring / growing others

**Mine it:** An intern or junior you ramped up. A struggling teammate you
quietly rescued. Onboarding docs or review culture you built.
**Story must show:** patience, tailoring to the person, and the *other
person's* trajectory — their outcome is your result.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 helps one person. L5 builds the thing that scales —
"the onboarding path I built cut ramp-up from three months to one" — or
grows someone into independence and then *gives them the credit*.

## Slot 7 — Deadline / prioritization under pressure

**Mine it:** A launch that couldn't move with scope that couldn't fit. Two
"top priorities" from two stakeholders. A production fire during crunch.
**Story must show:** explicit trade-offs, communicated early — "I told
stakeholders which features moved out and why" — and protecting the
must-not-break parts on purpose.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 works nights and ships. L5 renegotiates scope so
nights aren't needed: the senior version is a *decision* story, not an
*effort* story. If you did work the nights, the upgrade is what you changed
so the next launch didn't need them.

## Slot 8 — Proudest achievement / "biggest impact" (the wildcard)

**Mine it:** The work you'd show in a portfolio. Highest-blast-radius thing
you've shipped. This slot also backstops rare questions ("time you went
above and beyond", "creative solution").
**Story must show:** end-to-end drive — you spotted it, sold it, built it,
measured it — with your strongest numbers.
**Senior upgrade:** L4 is proud of hard code. L5 is proud of *outcomes and
leverage*: users affected, money saved, teams unblocked, and the moment
where the project would have died without your specific push.

## Delivery mechanics

- **Two-minute cap.** Rehearse each story to land at ~90–120 seconds. Long answers don't get cut short politely — they get cut short and cost you a story slot.
- **Then stop and let follow-ups come.** The interviewer's probes ("what did your manager think?", "what would you do differently?") are scheduled — they're how the story gets verified. Ending early and inviting them is confidence; filling silence is not.
- **Quantify or concretize every result.** Latency numbers, adoption counts, weeks saved, "became the team standard." Vague results score as no result.
- **First person.** Audit your drafts: every "we" that hides an "I" is thrown-away credit. Keep honest "we" for genuinely shared work — then immediately state your slice.
- **Close with the learning.** One reflection sentence, every story, even the triumphs.
- **Flex, don't recite.** In the room, listen to the *actual* question and lead with the part of your story that answers it. Your bank is eight sets of facts you know cold, not eight speeches.

## Rehearsal plan

1. **Days 1–8: one story per day.** Write the skeleton, then record yourself telling it (phone voice memo, camera off is fine). Listen back — you're checking for the 2-minute cap, "we"-hiding, and a missing reflection. Re-record once. This is uncomfortable, which is the point; discomfort now is calibration.
2. **Day 9: cross-mapping.** Make a grid: 8 stories × the archetypes each can serve. Every archetype needs ≥2 stories (interviewers ask "tell me about *another* time…"). Fill gaps with variant tellings, not new inventions.
3. **Day 10: mock round.** Have a friend (or a voice assistant, or a shuffled list) fire five archetype questions in random order with follow-ups: "What was the hardest part?" "What did the other person think?" "What would you do differently?" If a follow-up stumps you, the story needs another detail pass — real memories don't run out of detail.
4. **Ongoing: one story out loud per day** until the loop. Spoken fluency decays in days; the goal is that in the room you're *remembering events*, not remembering sentences.
